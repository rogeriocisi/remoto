Guide · Inside a simulation

What happens after you press Run.

Every iteration, in the order it runs: where the command comes from, how a spike crosses a synapse, what makes a membrane fire, how twitches become force and action potentials become EMG, and how the proprioceptors close the loop back onto the pool.

·How to read

One clock, and nothing else

ReMoto has no events waiting in a queue and no element that runs ahead of the others. There is a single clock, advancing in fixed steps, and on every tick each element of the model is updated once, always in the same order. Everything on this page is a consequence of that.

The step is part of the scenario, and in almost all the public ones it is 0.05 ms. A simulation of 5 s is therefore 100 000 iterations. Nothing in the model knows what time it is except through the number it is handed on each tick.

This page follows one iteration from end to end. If you only want to get a scenario running, the quick guide is the shorter way in; if you want to know which scenario does what, there is the list of the 45 public ones.

1Build

Pressing Run: what gets built

A scenario is a file, not a running thing. Pressing Run turns that description into objects in memory, in a fixed order, and only then does the clock start.

First the network: every motoneuron, interneuron and afferent fibre the scenario declares is created, one object each, with its own diameter, membrane resistance, capacitance, threshold and axon conduction velocity. These are not all equal. Within a pool the properties are graded from the first neuron to the last, which is what makes a nucleus a population and not a thousand copies of one cell.

Then the joint and the muscles, each with its motor units; the inputs, which are the descending tracts and any injected current; the nerve stimulation, if the scenario has any; and last the synapses, which are what connects all of it. Connections are drawn at random, with the probability the scenario gives, and the draw uses the scenario's seed.

That seed is the reason a run is repeatable: the same scenario with the same seed draws the same network and gives the same result, to the last digit, today or in five years. Each run stores the exact scenario, the seed and the engine version alongside its results.

Almost everything starts switched off. A scenario only creates what it declares, which is why an empty plot is usually an element that is not in the scenario rather than a bug.

2The iteration

One tick, in four movements

The loop is short enough to describe in a sentence: for each iteration, the time is the step times the iteration number, and then four things happen, always in this order.

  1. The nerves

    If the scenario has electrical stimulation, each active nerve is updated first. It decides whether a pulse falls on this tick and, if it does, which axons it excites — by their threshold, which depends on the fibre and on the stimulus intensity.

  2. The neurons

    Every neuron in the model is updated once: tracts generate their spikes, membranes are integrated by one step, thresholds are tested, axons move spikes along. This is where almost all the work is.

  3. The muscles

    Each musculotendon unit reads the joint angle to find its current length and moment arm, advances its motor units, computes the force, and feeds the proprioceptors.

  4. The joint

    Last, the joint collects the torques and advances. If it can move, the angle it reaches is the one the muscles will read on the next tick.

That order is the whole feedback loop in miniature. Neurons act on muscles within the tick; muscles act on neurons only on the next one, through the proprioceptors and the joint. The one step of delay is not an approximation to be apologised for — it is how a loop is closed in discrete time, and it is the same delay the published models had.

3Command

Where the firing comes from

A motoneuron in ReMoto does not decide to fire on its own. Something has to push it, and there are three ways in.

The usual one is a descending tract: a population of axons, each one a stochastic point process. The scenario sets the mean interval between spikes and the kind of process — Poisson, a gamma process of integer order, or Gaussian intervals. The order matters more than it looks: a Poisson command and a gamma command of the same mean rate produce very different force variability, because the order controls how regular the input is.

The mean rate does not have to be constant. It can follow a modulating signal — a ramp, a sinusoid, a square wave, an amplitude-modulated carrier, or an arbitrary series of points — which is how a scenario makes a contraction grow, oscillate or hold.

The second way is an injected current, applied to the soma or to the dendrite of a chosen pool, as in an experiment with an electrode. The third is nerve stimulation, which does not go through synapses at all: it excites the axon directly, and that is a different story, told further down.

4Synapse

How a spike crosses

A spike is an instant, not a current. What turns one into the other is the synapse, and it does so in three stages.

When a neuron fires, it hands the time of the spike to every synaptic group it transmits to. The group holds it for the conduction and synaptic delay of that particular connection, which differs from synapse to synapse.

When the delay is over, the spike releases a pulse of neurotransmitter, and the conductance of the group rises and then decays with its own kinetics. The model is the one that depends on transmitter concentration, so overlapping spikes do not simply add: the group aggregates them, in the way described by Lytton, which keeps the cost of a thousand synapses close to the cost of one while preserving saturation.

The conductance becomes a current only against the membrane potential of the moment: I = g × (E − V). That is why the same synaptic input does less as the cell depolarises, and why an inhibitory synapse can be silent at rest and strong during a contraction.

Synapses can also change with use. A group may be set to depress or to facilitate, so that a train of spikes does not deliver the same thing on the first and on the tenth. And the weight of a connection can fall with the distance between the two cells in the nucleus, which is how recurrent inhibition is kept local.

5The spike

The membrane, and the moment it fires

A motoneuron here has two compartments: a soma and a dendrite, joined by a coupling conductance. Both are integrated on every tick, the dendrite first, with the soma potential from the previous step, and then the soma, with the dendrite already updated.

Each compartment sums its currents — the ionic channels, the synapses, the leak towards rest, the current coming from the other compartment and any injected current — divides by the capacitance and advances the potential by a fourth-order Runge-Kutta step. The potential is clamped to a physiological range, as in the original model.

What fires is the soma. When its potential crosses the threshold, and the refractory period since the last spike has elapsed, the neuron registers a spike. Everything else follows from that one moment: the channels of the formulation in use respond, the spike enters the axon, and the time is handed to the synapses.

There are two formulations of those channels, and the scenario chooses. The original one swaps the rate constants for a pulse of fixed duration when the threshold is crossed, in the manner of the pulse-based models of Destexhe, and it is the default, so that published scenarios keep giving published results. The continuous one integrates the gating variables on every step, Hodgkin-Huxley style, and adds a persistent sodium current in the soma and an inactivating L-type calcium current in the dendrite. The difference that matters is not detail for its own sake: in the first, the persistent inward current is an event triggered by the soma; in the second it is a state of the membrane, which is what allows plateau potentials and self-sustained firing.

Interneurons have a single compartment and their own refractory period, but the rest of the story is the same.

6Axon

From the soma to the end plate

A spike at the soma is not yet a spike at the muscle. It has to travel, and the travel takes time that depends on the axon.

Each motoneuron has its own conduction velocity, graded across the pool, and from it come the latencies between the spinal cord, the site of stimulation and the end plate. A spike leaving the soma reaches the muscle a few milliseconds later, and the slowest units of a pool are measurably later than the fastest.

When the scenario stimulates a nerve, spikes also travel the other way. A stimulus excites the axon in the middle, and the resulting spike propagates both towards the muscle, which produces the M wave, and back towards the soma, antidromically. If an orthodromic and an antidromic spike meet on the same axon, they annihilate: that is the collision, and it is why the H reflex shrinks as the stimulus grows. Both are reproduced here because both are modelled, not because they were drawn.

A pool can also have a fraction of its axons blocked, counted from the slowest. Those motoneurons still fire at the soma — they appear in the raster — but nothing reaches the end plate and their motor units make no force. It is the conduction block of demyelinating disease, and it is the one place where the spikes you see and the force you measure deliberately disagree.

7Force and EMG

One spike, two signals

A spike arriving at the end plate produces two things at once, and they are computed separately all the way to the end.

The first is a twitch: a mechanical response with its own peak amplitude and time to peak, both properties of that motor unit. Slow units have small, long twitches; fast ones have large, brief ones. The twitch is the alpha function of the published model, advanced as a second-order system, so a unit firing repeatedly sums its own twitches and the force fuses as the rate climbs.

The second is a motor unit action potential: the electrical signature of that unit as seen by the electrode, with its own amplitude and time constant, and with an attenuation that depends on where the unit sits in the cross-section of the muscle. Deep units are seen faintly, superficial ones strongly.

The muscle force and the EMG are then the same operation applied to different quantities: the force is the sum of the twitches of every unit, and the raw EMG is the sum of the action potentials of every unit. Nothing else is added. What the results screen shows by default as EMG is that sum with noise and a band pass applied, the same way the old site presented it; the unfiltered sum is available too.

How the sum becomes a muscle force depends on the model the scenario chose. With the second-order critically damped system each unit contributes its twitch directly. With the Hill model the units drive an activation, and the activation drives a contractile element with its force-length and force-velocity properties, in series with a tendon — so the force depends on how long the muscle is and how fast it is changing, not only on how much it is being told to contract.

8The loop

The proprioceptors, and the way back

Up to here the signal has gone one way. What makes this a model of the spinal cord rather than a chain is that the muscle reports back, through two receptors, and the report arrives as spikes on the same pool that caused it.

The Golgi tendon organ reads the force in the tendon and drives the Ib afferents. It is a tension sensor: it responds to what the muscle is actually pulling with, which is not the same as how hard it is being driven.

The muscle spindle reads the length of the muscle, and also its velocity and acceleration, and drives the Ia and II afferents. It is the more elaborate of the two: it has its own intrafusal fibres — dynamic bag, static bag and chain — and its sensitivity is itself under control, through the fusimotor drive, which the scenario can hold constant or vary over time. A spindle with a high gamma drive reports a stretch that a slack one would not notice.

Both receptors turn their output into spikes the same way: the afferent fibre fires as a gamma process whose rate follows the receptor, conducts with its own velocity, and lands on the synapses of motoneurons and interneurons. From there the cycle starts again, one tick later.

Between the afferents and the motoneurons sit the interneurons, and they are what make the circuit a circuit: Renshaw cells, fed by the motoneurons themselves, inhibiting them back; Ia inhibitory interneurons, which silence the antagonist when the agonist is driven; and the Ib and group II interneurons through which tendon organ and spindle reach the pool. A stretch reflex, an H reflex and the oscillation of upright stance are all the same loop, read at different speeds.

9Joint

Where the force goes

Force in a muscle becomes torque at a joint through the moment arm, and the moment arm changes with the angle. ReMoto recomputes both on every tick, from polynomials fitted to the ankle, with the knee angle taken into account for the muscles that cross it.

What the joint does with that torque is the scenario's choice. Held isometric, it does not move: the length of each muscle is fixed and the loop through the spindle stays quiet unless something else stretches it. As an inverted pendulum, it is the model of upright stance: the body sways, the muscles are stretched by the sway, the spindles report it and the pool reacts — the full loop, with gravity as the disturbance. In a position task, it moves against a load. And with an imposed angle, the scenario drives the joint along a signal of its own, which is how a perturbation is delivered: a tendon tap or a ramp stretch, with everything downstream free to respond.

10Repeatable

Why two runs give the same numbers

A model with random connections, random spike trains and several threads has three good reasons to give a different answer every time. None of them applies here, and that is deliberate.

The randomness is seeded. Every draw — which neuron connects to which, when a Poisson axon fires, the noise on a conductance — comes from a generator started from the scenario's seed. Same seed, same draws, same run.

The parallelism does not change the order. Neurons are updated across all the cores of the machine, but a neuron that fires during a parallel step does not deliver its spike there: the spike is held and delivered afterwards, in the same sequence the single-threaded loop would have used. The number of threads changes how long a simulation takes and nothing else — results are identical, bit for bit, with one thread or with sixteen.

And the models are the published ones. The numerical core was ported from the original code with the requirement that every output stay identical, checked against reference hashes of the configurations of the old database. When a scenario of 2008 is run today, it gives what it gave then.

11Results

What comes out

While the loop runs, the elements that were asked to store signals write them down. What is kept is a choice, because keeping everything for every neuron of a large pool would not fit.

Always kept: the force of each muscle, the spikes of every neuron, the joint torque and the EMG. From those come the raster plots, the firing rates, the interval histograms and the force and torque curves.

Kept for the neurons you nominate — by default the first and the middle one of each type — the membrane potentials of soma and dendrite, each ionic and synaptic conductance, and the injected current. That is the level at which you watch a single cell charge, fire and recover.

Kept for the periphery: muscle and tendon lengths, the activation, the spindle and tendon organ outputs, the properties of each motor unit and its position in the cross-section. Every one of them can be plotted against time or against another, downloaded as CSV or as a figure, and the whole run can be exported with its configuration and seed.
